What This Is

A movement practice I can run for the rest of my life — from a single quiet day to the whole arc of ageing — that rebuilds physical capacity without cost, without dependence, and without sacrificing recovery.

The purpose in one paragraph

I am retraining a nervous system after injury and complex trauma, and rebuilding the physical competence a demanding role asks of me — strength, endurance, coordination, composure under stress, and the capacity to keep moving over rough ground. I do this on my own terms: quietly, predictably, and steadily, so my energy is freed for work, for the people I love, and for meeting others in their worst moments. This regimen is the physical half of my recovery. It sits alongside the Trauma Recovery Playbook and follows the same logic — stabilise first, expand gradually, integrate, then sustain.

Four commitments this is built on
Independence
No cost, no dependence
The core practice needs nothing but my body, the ground, a walk, and a loaded backpack. Everything beyond that is optional and bought once. No subscriptions, no apps, no fads.
Sustainability
The habit survives bad days
On days I’m unwell I scale down, I don’t stop. A defined minimum keeps the identity intact while protecting recovery. Rest, when it’s needed, is part of the training — not a failure of it.
Evidence
Grounded, not trendy
Every load, dose, and progression traces to a named, checkable source — major guidelines and primary research, listed on the Sources page. Nothing here is invented.
Fit
Inside the life I already live
It slots into my existing 5 AM start, my fuelling, and my weekend rhythm. It asks for consistency, not upheaval. Predictability is the point, not an obstacle.

Adapt covers sick days and the decades ahead. Review is how I keep myself honest. Each idea lives in exactly one place; pages link to each other rather than repeat.

Training Principles

The rules that decide every session. If a choice ever isn’t obvious, I return here.

1
Specificity — I get good at what I practise Motor learning canon Neural and physical adaptation mirrors the exact movements I train. So the practice must contain the things the role actually needs: carrying, climbing, getting off the ground, moving over terrain, staying composed while breathing hard. General fitness supports these; it doesn’t replace them.
2
Progressive overload — small, steady increments DeLorme 1945/48 Capacity grows when I ask slightly more than last time — a little more load, a rep, a minute, a harder variation — then let it consolidate. The origin of structured resistance training. The emphasis is on slightly: after nervous-system injury, the increment is smaller and the consolidation longer.
3
Dose is the active ingredient WHO 2020 The floor I aim to clear each week: 150–300 minutes of moderate aerobic movement (or 75–150 vigorous), plus strengthening of all major muscle groups on 2 or more days. That range is the target, not a pass/fail line — some is always better than none.
4
Recovery is where adaptation happens The session is the stimulus; sleep, food, and rest are where I actually get stronger. I do not train hard while sleep-deprived, ill, or acutely dysregulated — that trades long-term gains for short-term willpower. This is the hinge between this regimen and the Playbook’s hazards.
5
Variability & distributed practice Motor learning canon Skills consolidate better when practice is spaced out and slightly varied rather than crammed and identical. Little and often, across days, beats one long grind — and it’s gentler on a recovering system.
6
Effort I can name — and mostly keep sub-maximal Borg RPE I rate exertion on a simple scale and can hold a conversation through most aerobic work (the talk test). Easy days stay genuinely easy; hard days are deliberate and rare. Most of the volume lives in the comfortable-but-working range.
7
Minimum effective dose > heroic effort The smallest consistent practice that keeps me progressing beats the impressive one I can’t sustain. Showing up daily, even minimally, is the mechanism — not the size of any single session.
8
Self-compassion and scope-awareness are load management Judgement and forcing raise the nervous-system cost of every rep. Meeting myself where I am, and knowing the limits of a given day, isn’t softness — it’s what keeps the practice alive for decades.
One line to remember
Consistent, sub-maximal, specific, and recovered. Nearly every good decision in this regimen follows from those four words.

The Eight Qualities

The full map of what I’m training. Every session is some combination of these; nothing else needs to be added.

01
Aerobic base
Easy, conversational endurance — the foundation everything else rests on. Mostly walking and rucking.
02
Strength
Progressive force through the whole body. The single most protective quality across a lifetime.
03
Mobility & motor control
Full, controlled range at every joint; quality of movement, not just quantity of it.
04
Balance
Staying upright and reactive on uneven ground. Trainable at any age, and decisive later in life.
05
Power
Force applied quickly — jumping, throwing, exploding up. Added only once the base is solid.
06
Skill & unarmed self-defence
Awareness, distance, footwork, structure, and reactive coordination. Solo foundations are independent; live partner work is added later, with a qualified partner. No weapons.
07
Composure under load
Staying regulated while breathing hard or under mild stress — breath control paired with graded, self-titrated exposure. The bridge to performing when it counts.
08
Terrain & wilderness movement
Long walks and hikes over varied ground, carrying load, alongside navigation and first aid. My weekend already holds this.
How they sequence
Qualities 01–03 are the foundation and are trained from day one. Balance (04) threads through everything. Power (05), skill (06), and composure (07) are layered on only once the foundation holds — roughly Phase 2 onward. Terrain (08) is my weekend, always.

Phases

The build sequence. These are not deadlines — a phase ends when its criteria are met, however long that takes. The path winds; that is normal.

PhaseRough windowPriorityI move on when…
Phase 1
Stabilise & Groove		 ~Weeks 1–8 Rhythm before intensity. Daily walking, gentle full-body strength, mobility, and a movement I can trust. I’ve trained near-daily for several weeks with no flare-ups, and basic patterns feel controlled and painless.
Phase 2
Build Capacity		 ~Months 2–6 Progressive strength and aerobic volume. Balance work formalised. Load added to carries. Strength is climbing steadily, I clear the weekly aerobic floor comfortably, and recovery keeps pace.
Phase 3

Phase 4

A note on the one-year goal
Reaching high physical competence within a year is realistic if the foundation is respected. The fastest route through Phases 1–3 is refusing to rush Phase 1. A recovering nervous system rewards patience early with speed later.

Phase 1 — Stabilise & Groove

Roughly weeks 1–8. I’m not chasing gains yet. I’m building the daily rhythm and re-grooving basic patterns so my system learns that movement is safe and predictable.

Focus
Daily walkingGentle full-body strengthMobility every dayNasal-breathing paceConsistency > effort
The three building blocks
01 — Aerobic base
Walk daily, easy
Two walks a day where I can talk or breathe through my nose the whole time. Morning walk for light and circadian anchoring; a second later. Build toward the weekly aerobic floor WHO 2020 gradually.
02 — Strength
Six patterns, bodyweight
Squat, hinge, push, pull, carry, and brace — the movements that cover the whole body. Regressed as far as needed to be smooth and painless. 2 non-consecutive days to start.
03 — Mobility
Short daily flow
5–10 minutes of controlled range for hips, spine, shoulders, and ankles. This doubles as my warm-up and as a standalone on rest days.

Every set stops 2–3 reps short of failure. If a movement hurts or the pattern breaks down, I regress one step. No soreness beyond mild is the target early on — delayed soreness peaks a day or two after unfamiliar effort and is not a goal.

Exit criteria
I’ve moved near-daily for several weeks, the six patterns feel smooth and painless, and I’ve had no flare-ups or crashes I can trace to training. Then — and only then — Phase 2.

Phase 2 — Build Capacity

Roughly months 2–6. The foundation holds, so now I apply progressive overload in earnest — more load, more volume, and formal balance work.

What changes from Phase 1
Strength gets loaded and progressive
Add resistance (a kettlebell, dumbbells, or a heavier pack) and move toward harder variations. Log it, and nudge one variable up most weeks DeLorme 1945/48. Rep ranges settle around 5–12. Three sessions a week is typical.
Aerobic volume rises toward the range
Comfortably clear 150–300 min/week of moderate work WHO 2020, still mostly easy. Rucking — walking with a loaded pack — becomes the workhorse: it builds endurance and strength at once, for free.
Balance becomes deliberate
Single-leg stands, tandem walking, and reaching drills, woven into warm-ups and carries. Trainable at any age and worth banking now Otago programme.
First deloads appear
Every 4–6 weeks, an easy week at roughly half the usual volume lets adaptation catch up. Planned backing-off is not lost progress — it’s how progress consolidates. See Tracking.
Progression, concretely

Each week I try to move one variable, not all of them: a little more load, one more rep per set, one more set, a harder variation, a longer or heavier ruck, or slightly less rest. When several stall at once, that’s a signal to deload or check sleep and food, not to push harder.

Exit criteria
Strength benchmarks are climbing steadily, I clear the weekly aerobic floor without strain, balance has visibly improved, and recovery keeps pace with the load. Then Phase 3.

Phase 3 — Integrate & Perform

Roughly months 6–12. A strong, durable base now carries the sharper qualities — power, skill, and composure under load — and they start working together.

The three additions
05 — Power
Fast, low-volume, fresh
Jumps, med-ball or object throws, quick step-ups, kettlebell swings — a few crisp reps early in a session while fresh, never to fatigue. Power is quality; the moment it slows, I stop.
06 — Skill / self-defence
Awareness → footwork → contact
Solo: situational awareness, distance management, footwork, stance and structure, and reactive drills. Live partner work — graded, resistance-scaled — is added with a qualified partner once fundamentals are grooved. Unarmed only.
07 — Composure under load
Breath, then graded exposure
Deliberate breathing (long exhales, nasal work) practised at rest, then held while breathing hard or in mildly stressful drills I titrate myself. I stay inside my window of tolerance and back off before it closes.
Integration is the real goal
The point of Phase 3 isn’t any single quality — it’s their combination: carrying a load up a hill and still thinking clearly; moving skilfully while breathing hard; staying calm as effort rises. I practise them together, in short realistic pieces, not just in isolation.
Exit criteria
I can express strength, endurance, and skill together under mild stress without form or composure breaking down. That is functional competence — and it rolls straight into Phase 4.

Phase 4 — Sustain for Life

Year one onward. The build is done; now I maintain and refine indefinitely. This phase never ends — it only changes emphasis as I age.

What maintenance actually looks like

Maintaining a quality takes far less than building it. A steady week — the weekly template held consistently — keeps everything I’ve built. I keep progressing where I can and simply hold where I can’t. The practice becomes less a project and more just how I live.

Keep the stimulus, drop the strain
Strength stays progressive but unhurried; aerobic volume stays in the range; skill and balance stay in rotation. Hard days become rare and deliberate.
Guard against drift
Fitness fades measurably within weeks of stopping — aerobic capacity first, strength more slowly. This is why the minimum matters more than the maximum. A quiet, unbroken practice beats an intense, interrupted one.
Shift emphasis with age
The mix I train changes decade by decade — strength and balance grow in importance as protectors of independence. See Across the Decades.
Absorb life without breaking
Illness, travel, hard seasons — the unwell ladder and minimum dose keep the thread unbroken through all of them.

Daily Regimen

A default weekday, mapped onto the day I already keep. The movement is woven in, not bolted on — most of it lives in time I’m already using.

5:00 AM
Fuel, light, and the morning session Breakfast part one and tea as usual, then out for the sunlight walk — this is aerobic base and circadian anchoring in one. Back home for the day’s main movement block (strength, aerobic-mobility, or skill, per the weekly template), fuelled and fresh. See Fuelling.
Morning
Movement snacks through the work block The WHO guidance to sit less and break up inactivity WHO 2020 becomes a rep of something every hour or so: a short walk, a set of squats, a carry to the other room, a doorway mobility drill. Small, unremarkable, cumulative.
4:00 PM
Nature ritual — light second touch Paired with the last meal of the day, this window takes a lighter piece: an easy walk, mobility, balance drills, or solo skill work. Because it’s after my final meal, I keep it aerobic or skill-based rather than heavy — the demanding load belongs in the fuelled morning.
7:30 PM
Reset & wind-down Tidy and prep; an optional slow walking meditation. Nothing stimulating — the evening protects the sleep that protects tomorrow’s training.
8:00 PM
Evening routine → sleep The existing wind-down. Sleep is the highest-leverage recovery tool I have; I treat protecting it as part of the regimen, not separate from it.
The non-negotiable daily minimum
Even on the hardest day: one walk outside, a few slow breaths, and a short mobility flow. That’s the floor. It keeps the identity — I am someone who moves every day — intact, which is what carries the practice across years. Everything above it is a bonus, not a debt.

In Phase 1, drop to 2 strength days and keep everything easy. The shape is the same; the intensity isn’t there yet.

Equipment & No-Cost Core

What a full home practice actually requires. The honest answer: almost nothing. The core costs nothing and depends on no product, brand, or subscription.

Costs nothing
Always available

  • The floor, or a folded towel / rug as a mat
  • A sturdy backpack loaded with books or water jugs — my adjustable weight for carries, rows, squats, and rucking

Independence by design

Fuelling the Work

How the training fits my strict-keto kitchen. My existing menu already does most of this well; here is how to use it in service of movement.

What already works in my favour
Protein at every meal
Game meats, fish, poultry, and eggs at each meal give the steady protein that muscle repair and growth depend on — and that directly counters age-related muscle loss Sarcopenia lit.. This matters most on and after strength days.
Fat-adapted aerobic base
A fat-and-protein base suits the easy, conversational aerobic work that most of this regimen is built on. The morning session sits comfortably after breakfast part one; long easy walks and rucks are well fuelled by fat.
Built-in electrolytes
Bone broth with salt and lemon is already in my day. On keto, sodium needs run higher, and morning training adds sweat losses — broth and salt keep energy and training quality steady and head off the sluggish, low-sodium slump.
Omega-3–rich recovery
Sardines and salmon are regular staples; the omega-3s they provide support recovery from training load. No supplement required — it’s already on the plate.
Two things to be deliberate about
Objective caveat
Go easy on all-out intervals early
Very high-intensity, glycolytic efforts can feel harder and be somewhat blunted in a low-carbohydrate state; the evidence is mixed and individual. Practically: I favour the aerobic base and moderate-rep strength, introduce true high-intensity work conservatively, and make sure sodium and fuel are adequate first. This isn’t a limitation for most of what I train — the base and strength are unaffected.
Timing
Heavy work in the morning, light work at 4 PM
With the last meal at 4 PM, the evening is effectively fasted. So the demanding, loaded session belongs in the fuelled morning after breakfast part one, and the 4 PM touch stays aerobic or skill-based. This is already how my day is shaped.

Unwell & Low-Capacity Days

How I keep the habit alive without sacrificing recovery. On a hard day I don’t choose between “full session” and “nothing” — I step down one rung. The thread stays unbroken; the recovery stays protected.

The three-rung ladder
Green — well
Full session
Slept adequately, feeling steady, no illness or injury, regulated. I train the day as planned.
Amber — below par
Reduce, don’t skip
Poor sleep, high stress, something coming on, a flare, or feeling frayed. I swap intensity and load for an easy walk, a short mobility flow, deliberate breathing, and rest. Keep it brief and genuinely easy.
Red — ill or hurt
Rest is the training
Fever or active illness, an injury, or an acute crash or dysregulation. Structured training stops. Gentle only: a short slow walk if I’m able, warmth, breath, fluids, protein and electrolytes, and sleep. Returning early costs more than resting fully.
Why reducing beats skipping
Training hard while sleep-deprived, ill, or dysregulated trades long-term adaptation for short-term willpower — and it’s exactly the pattern the Playbook flags as a hazard. But dropping to zero on every off day erodes the identity that carries the practice. The amber rung keeps both: the streak and the recovery.
The minimum viable dose

On any day I think I “can’t,” the practice shrinks to something almost impossible to fail: one short walk outside, five slow breaths, and three easy mobility movements. It takes minutes and costs nothing in recovery. Its job isn’t fitness — it’s keeping I move every day true, so there’s no gap to climb back over tomorrow.

Coming back after a red stretch

I return one rung below where I left off, not where I was. After illness or a bad patch, the first week back is amber-by-default: rebuild the rhythm before the intensity. A missed week is a pause, not a reset — strength and skill fade slowly and return faster than they were first built.

Across the Decades

The same practice, re-weighted as I age. The through-line: as the years pass, strength and balance shift from things I build toward things that protect my independence — and resistance training remains the primary lever at every age.

The trajectory I’m training against
Left alone, muscle mass declines roughly 3–8% per decade after age 30, and faster after 60 Sarcopenia lit., and aerobic capacity (VO₂max) falls about 10% per decade VO₂max lit.. But both are substantially trainable: consistent training can roughly halve the aerobic decline, and resistance work builds muscle and strength at any age. The decline is real; so is my leverage over it.
EraEmphasisWhat changes
Now – 40s Build the ceiling Highest trainability. Bank strength, aerobic capacity, skill, and bone density now; peak the qualities the role needs. This is the cheapest decade to get strong in.
50s Preserve power Muscle and strength loss accelerate. Keep lifting with intent and keep some fast, low-volume power work; protect joints; hold VO₂max with easy volume plus occasional intervals; guard sleep and recovery harder.
60s Strength + balance forward Sarcopenia accelerates after 60 Sarcopenia lit.. Progressive resistance stays central; formal balance work moves up the priority list. Rucking gets lighter; skill and terrain continue.
70s+ Function & falls Strength and balance become non-negotiable for independence. A structured strength-and-balance programme of this kind reduces falls by roughly 35–40% in older adults Otago programme. Keep protein high, keep walking daily, scale the load — but never stop loading.
What never changes
The daily minimum, the unwell ladder, and “some is better than none” WHO 2020 scale down with me but never reach zero. At every age, the practice adapts to the body I have that decade — and keeps going.

Tracking & Self-Check

Enough measurement to steer by, and no more. Low-friction, no gadgets required, and honest — the numbers serve the practice, not the other way around.

Optional: resting heart rate on waking, and a one-line note on how sleep and recovery felt. The trend over months is the signal; any single day is noise.

Steering with the numbers
Rate effort, not just output
A simple perceived-effort scale Borg RPE keeps easy days easy and hard days deliberate. If “easy” sessions keep feeling hard, that’s a recovery flag, not a reason to push.
Deload on schedule and on signal
Every 4–6 weeks, an easy week at ~half volume. Also deload early if several benchmarks stall at once, sleep slips, or motivation drops — those cluster for a reason.
Review cadence

A brief monthly look at the benchmarks and the week-tallies; a deeper quarterly review of whether the phase still fits. This mirrors the Playbook’s monthly and quarterly reviews — I can run them together so recovery and training stay in step.

Evidence & Sources

Every evidence marker in this document points here. These are named, primary or authoritative sources I can verify independently — not summaries, and nothing invented.

The standard I held
I grounded each load, dose, and claim in a checkable source — a major guideline or primary research — identified well enough to look up. Where evidence is genuinely mixed (for example, ketogenic diets and high-intensity performance), I said so rather than overstating. I did not use the AI-generated background reports in this project as evidence; they are orientation only.
WHO 2020

PubMed ID: 33239350. Underpins the weekly aerobic range (150–300 min moderate / 75–150 vigorous), the ≥2-days strengthening floor, the older-adult balance-and-strength recommendation (≥3 days), “sit less,” and “some is better than none.”
DeLorme 1945/48

The origin of progressive-resistance / progressive-overload training, on which the strength progressions here are built.
Sarcopenia lit.

Freely readable via PubMed Central (PMC2804956). Source for muscle-mass loss of ~3–8% per decade after age 30, accelerating after 60, and for resistance training as the primary countermeasure at any age.
VO₂max lit.

Source for the ~10%-per-decade decline in VO₂max from around age 30 in average adults, and the finding that regular endurance training substantially slows that decline.

The Rating of Perceived Exertion scale, used here to keep easy days easy and hard days deliberate; the “talk test” is a practical proxy for moderate intensity.

One honest caveat
This is a general, evidence-based framework, not individualised clinical advice. Because I’m retraining after a nervous-system injury, a baseline read from a qualified professional makes the whole thing safer and smarter — not to medicalise my life, but as good reconnaissance before I load it.
